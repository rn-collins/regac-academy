What Agencies Actually Care About

How Regulatory Decisions Are Actually Made

Most regulatory compliance training teaches you the rules. Almost none of it teaches you how the people enforcing the rules actually think — what they read, what they flag, what makes a filing land versus get ignored.

After clerking in cannabis and psychedelics law during a period when federal/state conflicts were sharpening and DEA guidance was shifting in real time, the pattern became clear: organizations that understand the institutional logic of regulatory agencies do better than organizations that merely track the rules.

REGAC teaches that logic. Below is a preview of what that means in practice.

What most compliance training teaches

The rule changed. Here is what the new rule says. Here is the effective date. Here is the penalty for non-compliance.

What REGAC teaches

Why this rule changed. What the agency was responding to. What the comment period told them. What the next shoe is likely to be. How to write a filing that an agency attorney will actually read past page two.

What most AI compliance courses teach

GDPR applies. CCPA applies. The EU AI Act has tiers. Use an AI policy template.

What REGAC teaches about AI + regulation

How the FTC is actually assessing AI systems right now under frameworks designed for advertising and consumer protection — not AI. How the FDA thinks about AI-generated health claims. What "substantial evidence" means to a regulatory attorney reviewing an AI product. What to do before new legislation exists.

The AI Governance Track

Alongside the core regulatory intensive, REGAC offers a dedicated track for organizations deploying AI systems in regulated industries — cannabis, psychedelics therapeutics, health-adjacent, legal, financial services.

The track answers questions most AI governance frameworks don't ask: Not "what does our AI policy say?" but "how would an FTC attorney read your AI-generated health claim?" Not "do we have an AI policy?" but "what would the FDA need to see to take your AI-assisted clinical output seriously?"

Signal vs. Noise

What's Worth Reading Right Now

Signal

FTC enforcement actions against AI health claims (2024–2025)

The FTC is using existing Section 5 authority — not new AI legislation — to act on AI-generated health claims. The enforcement pattern reveals what "substantiation" means in practice before dedicated AI frameworks exist. Primary source: FTC.gov enforcement database.

Signal

DEA's proposed rulemaking on psilocybin scheduling (2024)

The comment period revealed the gap between what legal advocates submitted and what DEA staff were actually weighing. The pattern matters more than the outcome. Primary source: Federal Register, Docket DEA-2024-0029.

Noise

"AI Compliance Checklists" from vendors with AI products to sell

These are not compliance frameworks. They are liability-limitation documents designed to make you feel covered without creating enforceable obligations on the vendor. Read the actual regulatory guidance, not the summary a vendor wrote to accompany their product.

Noise

State-by-state cannabis compliance summaries from non-licensed sources

These go stale faster than they can be updated. Oregon Measure 110's collapse happened faster than most compliance summaries could track. The skill is reading primary sources, not consuming summaries of summaries.
